# 3‑D figures

In `lemur.anim`, 3‑D goes through a **`View`**: a camera, looking at a 3‑D
scene, drawing into a viewport on the slide. Curves, surfaces and solids made
through a view are genuinely three-dimensional. The deck stores their 3‑D
geometry once, and the player projects it for the current camera on every
frame. Orbiting the camera around a large mesh therefore costs only a few
numbers per keyframe.

```{lemur-example}
:step: 1
:files: three/orbit.py
:source:

!slide A ripple, from two sides

!anim
	!src orbit.py
```

## Two renderers

A view is drawn by one of two renderers, chosen when you make it:

- **`renderer="svg"`** (the default): the deck's player projects the view's
  shapes into SVG on every frame. Hidden parts are worked out against one
  convex occluder, a sphere or a dome (see [Hidden lines](#hidden-lines)).
  Solids are depth-sorted once.
- **`renderer="gpu"`**: the player draws the view with WebGL and a depth
  buffer, so anything hides anything, from any angle: a torus, a saddle, two
  objects in front of each other. Surfaces are single meshes that can have
  thousands of faces. Their outlines are smooth silhouettes computed for the
  current camera, and a `"hid"` curve is drawn exactly where the surfaces
  hide it.

```python
v = View(azim=-30, elev=30, scale=1.5, renderer="gpu")
```

```{lemur-example}
:files: three/torus.py
:source:
:step: 2

```

A GPU view never reaches paper as pixels:
- **Print and PDF** get an exact vector still: the faces painter-sorted for the
  final camera, the visible parts of every line, and the hidden parts dashed
  where asked.
- **The overview and browsers without WebGL 2** use the same vector still.

Labels, dots and 2‑D shapes stay crisp SVG on top of the canvas, and
`lmr2svg` writes the still at build time, so every copy of the deck prints the
same.

## The camera

```python
v = View(azim=-30, elev=25, scale=1.1, viewport=None, perspective=None)
```

| Parameter | |
|---|---|
| `azim`, `elev` | the camera's angles in degrees: around the z-axis, and above the xy-plane (z is up). `elev=90` looks straight down, which is how 2‑D plots are made |
| `scale` | drawing units per world unit (or use `v.fit(x_range, y_range)` to frame a data box) |
| `viewport` | `(cx, cy, w, h)`: a rectangle in the drawing area that the view draws into and is clipped to. Several views make insets |
| `perspective` | a camera distance for a perspective camera (nearer is bigger); `None` is orthographic |

`self.play(*v.reorient(azim=60, elev=55))` animates the camera. Several views
can share a scene: a main view and small top, front and side insets that all
follow the same moving point.

## Building blocks

| Method | Makes |
|---|---|
| `v.curve(points)`, `v.line(a, b)`, `v.parametric(fn, t0, t1)` | 3‑D curves |
| `v.curve_fn(points_fn)` | a curve recomputed every frame from `points_fn()`: one that grows, unrolls or follows a tracker |
| `v.axes(x, y, z, numbers=False)` | coordinate axes; `(lo, hi, step)` adds ticks |
| `v.surface(fn, u_range, v_range)` | a wireframe surface |
| `v.shaded_surface(fn, u_range, v_range, light=…)` | a lit, filled surface of small faces that turn away when they face backwards |
| `v.polygon(points)`, `v.solid(vertices, faces)`, `v.box(center, size)` | flat faces and solids, depth-sorted for the camera they start with |
| `v.dot(where)` | a dot at a 3‑D point (or at `where()`, if it is a function) |
| `v.label(text, anchor)`, `v.pin(shape, where, offset=…)` | upright text, or any 2‑D shape, pinned to a 3‑D point |
| `v.trace_dot(points, progress)`, `v.rate(points)` | a marker riding a curve, and a `Create` timing that keeps a growing curve's tip under it |
| `v.plot(fn, x0, x1)`, `v.area(fn, x0, x1)`, `v.point(x, y)` | 2‑D plots in data coordinates |
| `v.mesh(vertices, faces, colors, cull=True, edge=None, occlude=True)` | a surface of flat faces (GPU views): quads or triangles, one colour each; `occlude` makes it hide what is behind it |
| `v.contour(mesh, color, width)` | the outline of a mesh for the current camera: its smooth silhouette and open boundary (GPU views) |

In SVG views, solids are depth-sorted once, for the starting camera: keep them
still, and orbit wireframes and shaded surfaces, which stay correct from any
angle. In GPU views everything stays correct from any angle. There,
`shaded_surface` returns a single mesh, and `tint=` colours it by a function of
its parameters (curvature, say).

## Hidden lines

In a GPU view, the view's opaque meshes hide what is behind them. In an SVG
view, give the view an **occluder**, a solid sphere (or a dome standing on the
ground). Either way, the player works out what is hidden, for the current
camera and on every frame:

- `v.curve_fn(points_fn, rule="vis")` draws only the visible part of a curve,
  and `rule="hid"` only the hidden part. Drawn thin and dashed, the hidden part
  becomes a classic hidden line.
- `v.dot(where, rule="hide")` hides a dot behind the occluder;
  `rule="ghost"` dims it instead.
- `v.curve_fn(…, ghost=anchor_fn)` dims a whole curve while its anchor point is
  hidden.

```{lemur-example}
:step: 1
:files: three/sphere.py
:source:

!slide A great circle, front and back

!anim
	!src sphere.py
```

```python
v.occluder = {"c": [0, 0, 0], "R": 2.0, "cap": None}     # a sphere
v.occluder = {"c": [0, 0, 0], "R": 2.0, "cap": 0.0}      # a dome: the part above z = 0
```

For figures in the style of a geometry paper (a lit, opaque surface with its
silhouette and a contact shadow, and haloed curves and vectors), use the
ready-made vocabulary in [Illustrated figures](illustrated.md), which is built on
these rules.

## What ends up in the deck

A view's shapes are stored in the deck as 3‑D points:

- once, if they do not change;
- as a track, if they move;
- plus the view's camera angles over time.

The player projects them, splits curves at the occluder and hides or dims pins,
on every frame. The build does the same projection once for the deck's still
frame, which printing and the overview show. The file only grows with what
*changes*, not with how long the camera moves.

The examples `anim-3d`, `parallel-transport`, `tangent-space` and
`tangent-space-illustrated` show all of this at the scale of a lecture, and
`torus` shows GPU views: a torus seen from every side, coordinates, curvature
and geodesics.
